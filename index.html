Digital Sous Chef — a Claude + Notion build

Nothing that changes lives in the instructions anymore

I cook off recipe cards Claude builds for me. The version before this one lived in a Gemini gem, and I stopped opening it — not because the model wasn't good enough, but because everything it knew was trapped in a prompt I'd stopped looking at.

Why I quit the old one

It got confidently wrong, and nothing I liked ever accumulated.

Ask it for oatmeal, get a Mediterranean brunch. That wasn't a hallucination — it was a preference I'd written into the gem months earlier, quietly growing until it was the whole personality of the tool. It answered with total confidence, from instructions that had drifted away from how I actually cook.

And nothing stuck. My recipes lived in Paprika. The gem had no memory between chats, so it would invent a one-off dish, I'd cook it, it'd be good — and I could never find it again. I was spending more time managing the system than cooking with it.

Where everything lives now

Three pieces, three jobs, none of them instructions I have to maintain by hand.

The knowledge

Notion

Every recipe imported out of Paprika into a live database, plus what everyone at my table will and won't eat. It changes when my life changes, not when I remember to rewrite the instructions.

The rules

A skill

How a card gets built — layout, timing, where amounts go, how a dish splits. Written once, loaded on every card, regardless of which model is running.

The judgment

Claude

Reads both, adapts the recipe to what's in the fridge and who's eating, and writes the cook back to the library afterward so the next answer is better than this one.

What it actually does for me

I shop the sales first and figure out dinner second. This answers “what can I make with this?” every night.

It knows my favorites

Everything I've cooked and kept is in the library, searchable — along with where a dish needs to split for the mild eaters at my table.

It knows where I'd look

When the library doesn't have it, it goes to the cooking and food-science sites I actually trust instead of the open internet. If the dish is good, it goes into the library after I cook it.

One plan, not four recipes

Cooking three things at once, I don't flip between cards. It merges them into a single timing plan — what starts first, what holds, what fills the gaps.

This card was built by Haiku, start to finish. I asked for pulled pork. It pulled the recipe, worked out that the potato salad holds for hours and the pork doesn't, and put the salad in front as its own do-first phase — then used the 50-minute pressure window for everything that didn't need me at the stove.

None of that reasoning happened tonight. It happened once, months ago, in a much more expensive conversation, and then got written down.

Where the AI work actually belongs

Every one of those rules is a decision made once, on purpose, instead of re-litigated on a Tuesday night.

Design time

Reason it out once

The strong model earns its cost here: finding the failure, arguing about the fix, and turning the fix into a rule specific enough that a lighter model can't misread it.

Runtime

Just follow the rules

Nothing gets reinvented per card. The build is cheap, fast, and boringly consistent, because the thinking already happened and it's written down.

Getting there took stress-testing. I kept opening cards in my kitchen and finding small things that bugged me, and every fix surfaced the next one. What ended it was fixing them somewhere else — in the skill, as a permanent rule, instead of in the one card. The model was never the problem. Everything that came back wrong came back wrong because my spec allowed it.

I'd already learned this at work and didn't recognize it as the same thing.

I shipped a Gainsight Copilot agent that prepped business reviews. It tested beautifully and drifted in the field — formatting changed run to run, talking points dropped out, CSMs re-prompting just to get a complete script. It was making the job harder.

So I retired it and rebuilt the talking points as logic instead of instructions — a script running through Domo's AI agent against the account's own data, delivered alongside the dataset it came from, the same way every time. Prep went from an hour-plus to 15–30 minutes. Same move as the recipe cards: get the thinking out of the thing that runs, and into the thing that gets designed.

Put the knowledge where it can change, the rules where they can't drift, and the judgment where it belongs.

Digital Sous Chef — Claude + Notion. Knowledge in the database, rules in a skill, dinner made by me.
